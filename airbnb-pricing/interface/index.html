Step 1

Which data the model needs

The question is a regression problem: predict a number (price per night) from what a host knows about the listing. Three files from the Inside Airbnb Boston snapshot cover it.

listings.csv4,368 rows · 19 columns

One row per listing: location, room type, house rules, review activity, host size, license and the advertised price, which is the target. This is the modelling table.

neighbourhoods.csv26 rows

The official neighbourhood list. Used to validate the neighbourhood column and to fix the order of the 26 one-hot columns.

neighbourhoods.geojson26 polygons

Neighbourhood boundaries. They draw the maps and the location picker above. Harbor Islands has no listings, so it is shown in grey.

This summary file has no bedrooms, bathrooms, guest capacity, amenities or review scores. Titles give a bedroom count for only 39% of listings. Evaluation set-up

  • 80/20 split: 2,784 listings train the models and 696 are held back. The test set is scored only once, at the end.
  • 5-fold cross-validation on the training set for tuning and to check that results are stable.
  • Metrics in dollars: MAE (average $ off per night), RMSE (penalises big misses), R² on log price, MAPE, and the share of listings priced within ±20%.
Step 3

Five ways to predict a price

The methods range from a simple rule of thumb to ensembles of hundreds of trees. Each one makes a different assumption about how price relates to the features.

Baseline

The rule of thumb: charge the median of listings with the same neighbourhood and room type. Any real model has to beat it.

price = median(hood, room)
Learns
25 × 4 medians
Limit
Ignores everything else

Linear regression

Ordinary least squares. log(price) is a weighted sum of features, and each weight reads as a percentage effect on price.

log p = β₀ + Σ βᵢxᵢ
Strength
Transparent, fast
Limit
No interactions or curves

Lasso regression

The same linear model with an L1 penalty, which shrinks weak features to exactly zero. Cross-validation picked α = 0.001.

min ‖y − Xβ‖² + α Σ|βᵢ|
Strength
Selects features itself
Zeroed
4 features

Random forest

150 decision trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample with a random subset of features. The forest averages their answers.

p = mean(tree₁ … tree₁₅₀)
Tuned
depth 14, leaf 2
Strength
Interactions, stable

Gradient boosting

Small trees built one after another, each correcting what the previous ones got wrong.

p = f₀ + η Σ treeₖ

Step 5

Which method fits pricing best

Recommendation

Random forest sets the price; Lasso explains it.

  1. Most accurate. Random forest had the lowest cross-validated error and the lowest test MAE ($86/night). That is 33% less error than the neighbourhood-median rule of thumb.
  2. Captures interactions. A monthly minimum stay cuts the price of an entire home in Back Bay much more than a private room in Mattapan. Linear models can't represent that without hand-built interaction terms.
  3. Stable and low-maintenance. Gradient boosting comes within a dollar or two, but it needs more tuning and takes about 9× longer to train. The forest varies less from fold to fold.
  4. Lasso is kept for explanation. Lasso is about 20% less accurate, but its coefficients turn into plain statements such as "a private room is about 50% cheaper", which hosts trust. The calculator uses it for "Why this price".

Linear vs Lasso: why they tie

With 48 features and about 2,800 training listings there is little overfitting for Lasso to fix, so the best α is tiny and the two models score almost the same. Lasso still removed 4 neighbourhood dummies whose prices match the city average. The model gets simpler and no accuracy is lost.

Limits and next steps

  • Asking price, not booked price. The models learn what hosts ask. Adding calendar occupancy would let us optimise revenue instead of matching the market.
  • Missing property detail. Bathrooms, capacity, amenities and review scores from the detailed export would cut the error further. Today, just under half of test listings are priced within ±20%.
  • One snapshot. Boston prices peak around graduation, the marathon and fall foliage. A calendar-based model would add date effects.
  • Retrain each quarter on the new Inside Airbnb release.
Phase 2

A year of snapshots

Step 6

Four quarterly snapshots, two with prices

Phase 1 used a single snapshot (September 2026). Prices move through the year, so Phase 2 stacks four quarterly Inside Airbnb snapshots. Inside Airbnb published no prices for December and March, so those two quarters can't be scored. They still count: they show how many earlier quarters each listing was already live (its tenure), and the model prices their listing stock.

The March scrape is partial: about half the usual listings, skewed toward entire homes. Its higher predicted median reflects that mix, not a change in the market.

Phase 2 result

Pooling the quarters with time and zone cuts September error.

  1. A snapshot model goes stale. The June-only model over-prices September by and its error rises to /night.
  2. Using every quarter helps most. Pooling June and September does most of the work. Time features, zones and 2× weight on the newer quarter each trim a little more, reaching /night on September.
  3. Weights move between quarters. The license premium shrinks from summer to fall, and Downtown, Fenway and "view" listings hold their value better. One fixed set of weights can't serve the whole year.
  4. Some bias remains (about on September). With only two priced quarters, the model has one seasonal contrast to learn from. Priced December and March files would turn the quarter flag into a full season feature.
